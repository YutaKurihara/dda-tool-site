Flood extent and water depth

Sentinel-1 radar change detection maps where the water is; the FwDET algorithm then estimates how deep it is. One script (02_event/01_flood_detection.js) queues two batch tasks: the observed extent at 10 m, and the depth with its reliability flag at 30 m.

1 · Flood extent — Sentinel-1 change detection

Radar penetrates clouds, which is decisive during a flood event when optical satellites see nothing. The detector follows the UN-SPIDER recommended practice: open water is a smooth surface that reflects the radar pulse away from the sensor, so flooded ground turns dark relative to its pre-event state.

  1. Image selection — Sentinel-1 GRD, IW mode at 10 m, VV polarisation, filtered to the AOI. The only date the user enters is the flood date: the pre-event window is the 60 days that end six days before it (dry reference) and the post-event window the 12 days after it (during or after the flood); the lengths and the gap can be changed under Advanced settings. The gap matters because the rain that causes a flood arrives days before the peak, so a reference scene taken the day before is already wet and the ratio below would under-detect; six days is one Sentinel-1 repeat, short enough that every orbit still has a look inside the window. Ascending and descending passes are both searched. Each Sentinel-1 relative orbit is paired with itself — its own pre-event scenes against its own post-event scenes — so the two sides always share the same viewing geometry, and only orbits that observed the AOI in both windows take part.
  2. Orbit election — within each orbit the pre-event side takes, per pixel, the latest scene before the gap and the post-event side the earliest scene after the flood date. Both sides are composited the same way because the change threshold below was set for a one-scene-against- one-scene comparison. Since 2026-09-28 the orbits are ranked once for the whole AOI — by how much of it each orbit sees in both windows, ties broken by the earlier post-event scene — and the map is mosaicked by that rank: the primary orbit wherever it has data, the next only in its gaps. One viewing geometry and one date per region, clean seams, and "which orbit and date is this map" has an answer. The console prints the ranking and the primary orbit's share of the flood; the map shows days after flood per pixel. (The earlier per-pixel "closest scene wins" election remains available as orbitMode: 'closest'.)
  3. Speckle smoothing — a 50 m circular focal mean removes the salt-and-pepper noise inherent to SAR.
  4. Change test — the pixel is flagged as flooded when the backscatter drops by at least 3 dB: \( \sigma^0_{\text{pre}} - \sigma^0_{\text{post}} \ge 3\ \mathrm{dB} \). This replaced the UN-SPIDER ratio test (post/pre > 1.25 on the dB values) on 2026-09-27. The ratio is arithmetic on a logarithm: the drop it demands grows with pre-event brightness — 1.25 dB on bright land, 5 dB on dark — so it fired inside the natural 1–2 dB variability of bright land and demanded the impossible of dark land. Measured on Aceh Tamiang against a no-flood control date six days earlier, the false-alarm floor fell from 14 % to 2 % of the detection and hillslope contamination by 64 %. −3 dB is the literature value and the knee of the signal-to-noise curve; it was not chosen by looking at damage.
  5. Permanent water — pixels that are water in a normal year are removed: JRC Global Surface Water v1.4, seasonality ≥ 10 months (the UN-SPIDER rule) or occurrence ≥ 80 %. The second band fills the no-data gaps the first leaves under persistent cloud. Floodplains that flood a few weeks a year sit far below either bar and are kept.
  6. Refinement — clusters smaller than 50 pixels (0.5 ha) are removed with a connected-pixel filter, and terrain steeper than 5° (where radar shadow mimics water) is masked. The slope comes from FABDEM at 30 m since 2026-09-27: the 464 m HydroSHEDS DEM used before averages a mountainside below 5°, leaving 8 % of the mask on ground FABDEM calls steep. The result is pinned to a fixed 10 m grid so that it does not change with zoom level.

Three classes, not two: flood / dry / unknown

A binary mask asserts that everything not detected is dry, and the depth model below then reads the DEM along every edge of it as a shoreline. Where that edge is a land-cover boundary — canopy, or built-up land the C-band radar cannot see through — and not the water's edge, the ground does not rise there and the water-level reference is lost. A synthetic test with the DEM error cancelled exactly recovered 98 % of a known depth from a hole-free extent and only 3–7 % once the measured hole pattern was punched into it. So since 2026-09-27 every pixel is classified as flood, dry or unknown — the same idea as the Copernicus GFM exclusion mask — and only confidently dry land may act as a boundary. A pixel is unknown when any of these holds:

  • Enclosure — a non-flood pixel with flood on at least half of the ground within 300 m is an internal hole of the detected lace, not a shoreline, whatever its backscatter. This is the clause that recovers the depth.
  • Brightness — pre-event VV at or above −7 dB is canopy or built-up land; the radar cannot rule out water beneath it. Auxiliary, but the only clause that reaches buildings.
  • Permanent water — a channel is water, never a dry boundary.
  • Never observed in both windows.

A confident detection is never unknown. The preview shows the three classes as an Observability layer and the console prints their areas.

Why permanent water is removed twice — and once not at all. Lake surfaces are not stable in radar: wind roughens them by 10 dB or more, which is larger than the darkening a real flood produces, so a calm post-event lake reads as "flooded" unless it is masked. The mask is applied to the reported extent. It is not applied to the extent handed to the depth model: FwDET reads the water level off the flood's edge, and a DEM records a lake at its normal-stage surface, so cutting a lake out first would turn its shore into a false edge that drags the surrounding depths down (the underestimation Cohen et al. report at Lake Houston). Depth is computed on the unmasked extent and masked afterwards, matching the extent.

Alternative input: pre-analysed flood polygons

When a flood map already exists (drone survey, provincial GIS office, UNOSAT), it can be uploaded as an Earth Engine table and rasterised onto the same 10 m grid instead of running the SAR detection. Everything downstream — depth, valuation, dashboard — is identical.

2 · Water depth — FwDET on a bare-earth DEM

Damage curves need depth, not just extent. The Floodwater Depth Estimation Tool (FwDET, Cohen et al. 2018, v2.1) reconstructs the water surface from the flood boundary:

  1. Take the ground elevation along the flood-extent boundary — by definition the water surface elevation there.
  2. Interpolate that boundary elevation across the interior of the flooded area (implemented with a cumulative-cost spread in Earth Engine).
  3. Subtract the DEM: \( \text{depth} = \text{water surface} - \text{ground elevation} \)

The DEM is FABDEM (30 m): Copernicus GLO-30 with forest canopy and building heights removed by machine learning (Hawker et al. 2022). FwDET needs bare-earth ground elevation — a surface model that includes rooftops and tree crowns would systematically distort depths in exactly the urban and vegetated areas that matter most. The depth is computed on FABDEM's own 30 m grid; 10 m was oversampling it.

Three things about how the algorithm runs here. The boundary is taken from confidently dry land only — unknown pixels are dropped from it, so the interpolation reaches past a canopy edge to a boundary that is plausibly the water's edge. The reach of that interpolation is 5 km, raised from 1 km on 2026-09-28 once the unknown edges were gone and the core of a large flood — the most certainly flooded place on the map — was being left without a surface. And where the interpolated surface rests on essentially no dry land within its reach (under 3 % of the neighbourhood), the depth is masked rather than invented, so that "depth unknown" and "0 m" stay distinguishable downstream.

Inferred flood

An unknown pixel whose terrain sits below the estimated water surface is inferred to be flooded (decision of 2026-09-28). It is an inference from terrain, not a satellite observation, so it is carried separately: the depth Asset's depth_flag band records 1 for depth from a valid boundary, 2 for inferred, 3 for no valid boundary (depth masked). The reported flood extent is observed OR inferred, and that union is made when the Assets are read (C.floodExtentImage()), not when they are written — see Outputs.

Why the mask no longer carries the inferred pixels (2026-10-16). Until then the mask Asset was written as observed ∪ inferred — which meant the mask task had to run the whole depth model, province-wide, only to burn depth_flag == 2 into a 10 m raster, and the depth task then ran it again. Aceh's mask export was still running after 148 hours. The definition of the extent has not changed; the union is now made on read by one accessor that every consumer uses, and the mask task finishes in the time a Sentinel-1 change detection takes. Until the depth task lands the dashboard shows the observed extent and flags DEPTH PENDING; run Stage 2 only once both Assets exist.
