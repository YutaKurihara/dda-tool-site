Flood extent and water depth

Sentinel-1 radar change detection maps where the water is; the FwDET algorithm then estimates how deep it is. Both rasters are produced at 10 m in a single export (02_event/01_flood_detection.js).

1 · Flood extent — Sentinel-1 change detection

Radar penetrates clouds, which is decisive during a flood event when optical satellites see nothing. The detector follows the UN-SPIDER recommended practice: open water is a smooth surface that reflects the radar pulse away from the sensor, so flooded ground turns dark relative to its pre-event state.

  1. Image selection — Sentinel-1 GRD, IW mode, VV polarisation, single orbit direction, filtered to the AOI. Two mosaics are built: a pre-event window (dry reference) and a post-event window (during/after the flood).
  2. Speckle smoothing — a focal median removes the salt-and-pepper noise inherent to SAR.
  3. Change ratio — the pixel is flagged as flooded when \( \sigma^0_{\text{pre}} / \sigma^0_{\text{post}} > 1.25 \) (backscatter dropped by ≥ 25 %).
  4. Refinement — isolated pixel clusters are removed with a connected-pixel filter, and steep terrain (where radar shadow mimics water) is masked using a slope threshold.
Design choice: the JRC permanent-water mask is deliberately not subtracted. River channels overflowing their banks are part of the flood; removing "permanent water" would cut holes exactly where the flooding started.

Alternative input: pre-analysed flood polygons

When a flood map already exists (drone survey, provincial GIS office, UNOSAT), it can be uploaded as an Earth Engine table and rasterised onto the same 10 m grid instead of running the SAR detection. Everything downstream — depth, valuation, dashboard — is identical.

2 · Water depth — FwDET on a bare-earth DEM

Damage curves need depth, not just extent. The Floodwater Depth Estimation Tool (FwDET, Cohen et al. 2018, v2.1) reconstructs the water surface from the flood boundary:

  1. Take the ground elevation along the flood-extent boundary — by definition the water surface elevation there.
  2. Interpolate that boundary elevation across the interior of the flooded area (implemented with a cumulative-cost spread in Earth Engine).
  3. Subtract the DEM: \( \text{depth} = \text{water surface} - \text{ground elevation} \)

The DEM is FABDEM (30 m): Copernicus GLO-30 with forest canopy and building heights removed by machine learning (Hawker et al. 2022). FwDET needs bare-earth ground elevation — a surface model that includes rooftops and tree crowns would systematically distort depths in exactly the urban and vegetated areas that matter most.
