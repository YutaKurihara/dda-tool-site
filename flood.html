Flood extent and water depth

Sentinel-1 radar change detection maps where the water is; the FwDET algorithm then estimates how deep it is. Both rasters are produced at 10 m in a single export (02_event/01_flood_detection.js).

1 · Flood extent — Sentinel-1 change detection

Radar penetrates clouds, which is decisive during a flood event when optical satellites see nothing. The detector follows the UN-SPIDER recommended practice: open water is a smooth surface that reflects the radar pulse away from the sensor, so flooded ground turns dark relative to its pre-event state.

  1. Image selection — Sentinel-1 GRD, IW mode at 10 m, VV polarisation, single orbit direction, filtered to the AOI. Two composites are built: a pre-event window reduced to its median (so that one windy or wet scene cannot set the dry reference), and a post-event mosaic that keeps the peak of the flood.
  2. Speckle smoothing — a 50 m circular focal mean removes the salt-and-pepper noise inherent to SAR.
  3. Change ratio — the pixel is flagged as flooded when \( \sigma^0_{\text{post}} / \sigma^0_{\text{pre}} > 1.25 \). Both values are in decibels and negative over land, so a ratio above one means the surface became darker. The test is UN-SPIDER's as published; note that on dB values the drop it demands grows with the pre-event brightness.
  4. Permanent water — pixels that are water in a normal year are removed: JRC Global Surface Water v1.4, seasonality ≥ 10 months (the UN-SPIDER rule) or occurrence ≥ 80 %. The second band fills the no-data gaps the first leaves under persistent cloud. Floodplains that flood a few weeks a year sit far below either bar and are kept.
  5. Refinement — clusters smaller than 50 pixels (0.5 ha) are removed with a connected-pixel filter, and terrain steeper than 5° (where radar shadow mimics water) is masked. The result is pinned to a fixed grid so that it does not change with zoom level.
Why permanent water is removed twice — and once not at all. Lake surfaces are not stable in radar: wind roughens them by 10 dB or more, which is larger than the darkening a real flood produces, so a calm post-event lake reads as "flooded" unless it is masked. The mask is applied to the reported extent. It is not applied to the extent handed to the depth model: FwDET reads the water level off the flood's outer edge, and cutting a lake out first would turn its shore into a false edge at the lake's own elevation, dragging the depths around it down. Depth is therefore computed on the unmasked extent and masked afterwards, matching the extent.

Alternative input: pre-analysed flood polygons

When a flood map already exists (drone survey, provincial GIS office, UNOSAT), it can be uploaded as an Earth Engine table and rasterised onto the same 10 m grid instead of running the SAR detection. Everything downstream — depth, valuation, dashboard — is identical.

2 · Water depth — FwDET on a bare-earth DEM

Damage curves need depth, not just extent. The Floodwater Depth Estimation Tool (FwDET, Cohen et al. 2018, v2.1) reconstructs the water surface from the flood boundary:

  1. Take the ground elevation along the flood-extent boundary — by definition the water surface elevation there.
  2. Interpolate that boundary elevation across the interior of the flooded area (implemented with a cumulative-cost spread in Earth Engine).
  3. Subtract the DEM: \( \text{depth} = \text{water surface} - \text{ground elevation} \)

The DEM is FABDEM (30 m): Copernicus GLO-30 with forest canopy and building heights removed by machine learning (Hawker et al. 2022). FwDET needs bare-earth ground elevation — a surface model that includes rooftops and tree crowns would systematically distort depths in exactly the urban and vegetated areas that matter most.
