Flood Mapping and Damage Assessment

The tool uses Sentinel-1 radar images to map flooded areas and elevation data to estimate floodwater depth. The results are combined with buildings, roads, agricultural land and other infrastructure data to estimate flood impacts and direct physical damage. Satellite-based flood mapping can support rapid assessment, particularly when field access is difficult.

What the tool provides

  • Flood extent — areas detected as flooded from satellite observations.
  • Inferred flood extent — additional areas estimated to be flooded using terrain and water-level information.
  • Flood depth — estimated water depth in metres.
  • Damage estimates — potential direct physical damage to buildings, infrastructure, agriculture and other exposed assets, with affected population and facility counts.

How it works

  1. Select satellite images. Sentinel-1 radar images before and after the flood are selected for the affected area. The user enters one date — the flood date — and the tool picks the reference and post-flood windows around it.
  2. Detect flooded areas. Open water reflects the radar pulse away from the sensor, so newly flooded ground turns dark. Changes in the radar signal are used to identify flooded areas; permanent water bodies and terrain likely to produce false detections are excluded.
  3. Estimate water depth. The FwDET method uses the flood boundary and a bare-earth elevation model to estimate the water surface and the depth beneath it.
  4. Estimate damage. Flood extent and depth are combined with asset locations, replacement costs and depth–damage relationships to estimate direct physical damage, affected people and affected facilities.

Reading the results: observed, inferred, unknown

Not every pixel inside a flood map carries the same kind of evidence. The tool keeps the distinction explicit, and so should any report built on it.

ClassMeaningHow to use it
Observed floodFlooding detected in the radar image.Direct evidence. Omissions under vegetation and in built-up areas, and occasional false detections, are possible.
Inferred floodGround the radar could not decide on, but whose elevation lies below the estimated water surface.An estimate from terrain, not a satellite observation. It is part of the reported extent and is labelled separately in the data.
UnknownAreas where the observation conditions do not allow a decision — dense canopy, built-up land, permanent water, or no coverage.Do not read as dry. Unknown areas are excluded from the depth calculation and should be checked in the field where they matter.
Estimated depthWater depth computed from the flood boundary and the elevation model.A model estimate, not a measured water level.
Unknown is not dry. A binary flood map silently calls everything it did not detect "dry". This tool does not: canopy and built-up land the C-band radar cannot see through are reported as unknown, which is why flooding beneath them can still appear as inferred flood when the water surface says it must be there.

Accuracy and limitations

  • Sentinel-1 can observe the ground through clouds, but flooding under dense vegetation or in built-up areas may be difficult to detect.
  • Some flooded areas are inferred from terrain rather than directly detected by the satellite.
  • Water depth is estimated using elevation data and may differ from actual floodwater depth. It has not yet been validated against measured water levels; read it as an indication of relative depth rather than a measurement.
  • Flood maps represent conditions around the satellite observation dates, not necessarily the maximum flood extent.
  • Damage estimates are preliminary direct-damage estimates and should be checked against field observations where available.

Technical details

Everything below is for analysts who need to know exactly what the scripts do. One script (02_event/01_flood_detection.js) queues two batch tasks: the observed extent at 10 m, and the depth with its reliability flag at 30 m.

T1 · Flood extent — Sentinel-1 change detection

The detector follows the UN-SPIDER recommended practice in outline: open water is a smooth surface that reflects the radar pulse away from the sensor, so flooded ground turns dark relative to its pre-event state.

  1. Image selection — Sentinel-1 GRD, IW mode at 10 m, VV polarisation, filtered to the AOI. The only date the user enters is the flood date: the pre-event window is the 60 days that end six days before it (dry reference) and the post-event window the 12 days after it (during or after the flood); the lengths and the gap can be changed under Advanced settings. The gap matters because the rain that causes a flood arrives days before the peak, so a reference scene taken the day before is already wet and the change test would under-detect; six days is one Sentinel-1 repeat, short enough that every orbit still has a look inside the window. Ascending and descending passes are both searched. Each Sentinel-1 relative orbit is paired with itself — its own pre-event scenes against its own post-event scenes — so the two sides always share the same viewing geometry, and only orbits that observed the AOI in both windows take part.
  2. Orbit election — within each orbit the pre-event side takes, per pixel, the latest scene before the gap and the post-event side the earliest scene after the flood date. Both sides are composited the same way because the change threshold below was set for a one-scene-against- one-scene comparison. The orbits are ranked once for the whole AOI — by how much of it each orbit sees in both windows, ties broken by the earlier post-event scene — and the map is mosaicked by that rank: the primary orbit wherever it has data, the next only in its gaps. One viewing geometry and one date per region, clean seams, and "which orbit and date is this map" has an answer. The console prints the ranking and the primary orbit's share of the flood; the map shows days after flood per pixel. (A per-pixel "closest scene wins" election remains available as orbitMode: 'closest'.)
  3. Speckle smoothing — a 50 m circular focal mean removes the salt-and-pepper noise inherent to SAR.
  4. Change test — the pixel is flagged as flooded when the backscatter drops by at least 3 dB: \( \sigma^0_{\text{pre}} - \sigma^0_{\text{post}} \ge 3\ \mathrm{dB} \). −3 dB is the literature value and the knee of the signal-to-noise curve; it was not chosen by looking at damage. (Why a dB difference rather than the UN-SPIDER ratio: see the changelog.)
  5. Permanent water — pixels that are water in a normal year are removed: JRC Global Surface Water v1.4, seasonality ≥ 10 months (the UN-SPIDER rule) or occurrence ≥ 80 %. The second band fills the no-data gaps the first leaves under persistent cloud. Floodplains that flood a few weeks a year sit far below either bar and are kept.
  6. Refinement — clusters smaller than 50 pixels (0.5 ha) are removed with a connected-pixel filter, and terrain steeper than 5° (where radar shadow mimics water) is masked, using the slope of FABDEM at 30 m. The result is pinned to a fixed 10 m grid so that it does not change with zoom level.

T2 · Three classes, not two: flood / dry / unknown

A binary mask asserts that everything not detected is dry, and the depth model below then reads the DEM along every edge of it as a shoreline. Where that edge is a land-cover boundary — canopy, or built-up land the C-band radar cannot see through — and not the water's edge, the ground does not rise there and the water-level reference is lost. A synthetic test with the DEM error cancelled exactly recovered 98 % of a known depth from a hole-free extent and only 3–7 % once the measured hole pattern was punched into it. So every pixel is classified as flood, dry or unknown — the same idea as the Copernicus GFM exclusion mask — and only confidently dry land may act as a boundary. A pixel is unknown when any of these holds:

  • Enclosure — a non-flood pixel with flood on at least half of the ground within 300 m is an internal hole of the detected lace, not a shoreline, whatever its backscatter. This is the clause that recovers the depth.
  • Brightness — pre-event VV at or above −7 dB is canopy or built-up land; the radar cannot rule out water beneath it. Auxiliary, but the only clause that reaches buildings.
  • Permanent water — a channel is water, never a dry boundary.
  • Never observed in both windows.

A confident detection is never unknown. The preview shows the three classes as an Observability layer and the console prints their areas.

Why permanent water is removed twice — and once not at all. Lake surfaces are not stable in radar: wind roughens them by 10 dB or more, which is larger than the darkening a real flood produces, so a calm post-event lake reads as "flooded" unless it is masked. The mask is applied to the reported extent. It is not applied to the extent handed to the depth model: FwDET reads the water level off the flood's edge, and a DEM records a lake at its normal-stage surface, so cutting a lake out first would turn its shore into a false edge that drags the surrounding depths down (the underestimation Cohen et al. report at Lake Houston). Depth is computed on the unmasked extent and masked afterwards, matching the extent.

T3 · Alternative input: pre-analysed flood polygons

When a flood map already exists (drone survey, provincial GIS office, UNOSAT), it can be uploaded as an Earth Engine table and rasterised onto the same 10 m grid instead of running the SAR detection. Everything downstream — depth, valuation, dashboard — is identical.

T4 · Water depth — FwDET on a bare-earth DEM

Damage curves need depth, not just extent. The Floodwater Depth Estimation Tool (FwDET, Cohen et al. 2018, v2.1) reconstructs the water surface from the flood boundary:

  1. Take the ground elevation along the flood-extent boundary — by definition the water surface elevation there.
  2. Interpolate that boundary elevation across the interior of the flooded area (implemented with a cumulative-cost spread in Earth Engine).
  3. Subtract the DEM: \( \text{depth} = \text{water surface} - \text{ground elevation} \)

The DEM is FABDEM (30 m): Copernicus GLO-30 with forest canopy and building heights removed by machine learning (Hawker et al. 2022). FwDET needs bare-earth ground elevation — a surface model that includes rooftops and tree crowns would systematically distort depths in exactly the urban and vegetated areas that matter most. The depth is computed on FABDEM's own 30 m grid; 10 m was oversampling it.

Three things about how the algorithm runs here. The boundary is taken from confidently dry land only — unknown pixels are dropped from it, so the interpolation reaches past a canopy edge to a boundary that is plausibly the water's edge. The reach of that interpolation is 5 km, so that the core of a large flood — the most certainly flooded place on the map — is not left without a surface. And where the interpolated surface rests on essentially no dry land within its reach (under 3 % of the neighbourhood), the depth is masked rather than invented, so that "depth unknown" and "0 m" stay distinguishable downstream.

T5 · Inferred flood

An unknown pixel whose terrain sits below the estimated water surface is inferred to be flooded. It is an inference from terrain, not a satellite observation, so it is carried separately: the depth Asset's depth_flag band records 1 for depth from a valid boundary, 2 for inferred, 3 for no valid boundary (depth masked). The reported flood extent is observed OR inferred, and that union is made when the Assets are read (C.floodExtentImage()), not when they are written.
