Forest fire — VIIRS active-fire detection

Thermal detection of burning, read from the satellite feed for all 514 Kabupaten/Kota every day and split by forest cover (03_dashboard/02_forestfire.js). Unlike the other three hazards there is no Stage-1 detector and no stored result: a fire season is a stream that updates daily, not an event with a footprint.

1 · Detection logic

Optical sensors see smoke; thermal sensors see the fire beneath it. That is decisive in Indonesia, where the fire season is the cloud season.

  1. Image selection — VIIRS near-real-time active fire at 375 m, from both platforms merged: NASA/LANCE/SNPP_VIIRS/C2 and NASA/LANCE/NOAA20_VIIRS/C2, filtered to Indonesia. Two satellites roughly double the overpasses per day.
  2. Detection — each image is a daily raster. A pixel counts when Bright_ti4 > 0 and the detection is of nominal or high confidence. Dropping the low-confidence class matters: that is where gas flares, hot industrial roofs and cloud-edge artefacts live, and it is why an all-urban province like DKI Jakarta was reporting burned area at all.
  3. Accumulation — over the selected period, every pixel that fired at least once is kept. A pixel burning for five days running is one burned pixel, not five.
  4. Forest test — a burned pixel counts as forest when the in-house Random-Forest land-cover map (2025, 30 m) says forest at that pixel's centre.

2 · From detections to burned area

One VIIRS pixel is 375 m × 375 m = 0.140625 km². Burned area is the count of distinct detected pixels times that constant:

$$\text{burned area}\;[\mathrm{km^2}] \;=\; N_{\text{distinct pixels}} \;\times\; 0.140625$$

Each city gets two figures — total burned area and the part of it that was forest — plus the share of its own forest that has gone. The forest denominator is sampled rather than summed: one 30 m reading stands for each 250 m cell. That is unbiased for an area total and costs a fraction of reading all 2.1 billion 30 m pixels of Indonesia, which would be a batch export rather than a dashboard.

4 · Limits to keep in mind

Why this is not a burn perimeter. A perimeter is an outline of ground that burned, traced from before-and-after imagery. VIIRS reports something different: at each overpass, which 375 m cells contained fire hot enough to detect. The figure above is the combined footprint of those cells — where fire was seen, not what ground burned.

That difference cuts both ways. One burning hectare flags its entire 375 m cell, all 14 ha of it, so small fires are over-stated. And a fire that burned between two overpasses, or sat under cloud every time the satellite looked, is never detected at all — its scar is simply missing. Read the number as where and when fire is concentrated, not as an area of burned land.
  • No burn severity. Sentinel-2 dNBR needs a clear before-and-after pair. Over a month-long window in Kalimantan the optical composite is mostly cloud holes, and the "low severity" class fills up with ordinary vegetation change instead of fire — so this tool does not produce a severity map at all.
  • The smallest Kabupaten have the noisiest forest figures. Forest area is sampled, so a city's accuracy depends on how many sample cells fall inside it. A large regency contains thousands; a small kota contains few, and its forest-share column should be read as approximate.
  • The satellite feed runs a day or more behind. NASA's near-real-time service (LANCE) publishes with a lag, so the daily chart stops at the last day actually published rather than at today. A trailing point drawn at zero would say "the fires stopped" when it means "nobody has looked yet".

5 · Display and outputs

The period. Two controls at the top of the left column set it: a picker holding either the live 30-day window or one of the last six years, and a slider whose twelve stops are January to December. Everything below follows them together — the map, the KPI row, the province ranking and all three charts. A screen showing July on the map and "the last 30 days" in the numbers beside it would be misread, so that state cannot be reached.

Hotspot colours. Detections are shaded by how recently each pixel first burned, newest to oldest: violet → red → orange → yellow. In the live window the four classes are under 1 day, 1–3 days, 3–7 days and 7 days or older. A past month has no "days ago" to measure, so its elapsed days are divided into four equal parts instead and each pixel takes the colour of the part it first burned in — the month's last quarter violet, its first quarter yellow. The map therefore reads the same either way, and only the legend's wording changes.

Charts and export. Three time series — daily within the selected period, monthly across the year, yearly across the last six. Clicking a Kabupaten/Kota on the map re-scopes all of them to that city. The ranking table lists 34 provinces rather than 514 cities, because at national scope a ranking of every city buries the answer; every city is still in the CSV export.
