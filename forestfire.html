Forest fire — a live national monitor

The screen above (03_dashboard/02_forestfire.js) answers one question continuously: where is it burning in Indonesia, and how much forest is going. It reads VIIRS active-fire detections for all 514 Kabupaten/Kota, every day, and separates the share that falls on forest.

This one is not an event pipeline. Floods, landslides and earthquakes are events: something happens, Stage 1 maps its extent, Stage 2 prices it, and the result is a stored table. A fire season is not an event. It is a stream that updates daily for months, and there is no moment at which you would freeze it and call it the answer. So this screen has no Stage-1 detector and no Stage-2 valuation behind it — it reads the satellite feed directly and computes nothing that persists.

1 · What it detects

VIIRS active fire, 375 m — the near-real-time product from both platforms, merged: NASA/LANCE/SNPP_VIIRS/C2 and NASA/LANCE/NOAA20_VIIRS/C2. Each image is a daily raster; a pixel with Bright_ti4 > 0 is a detection. Two satellites rather than one roughly doubles the overpasses, which matters on an island where the fire and the cloud are the same season.

Thermal detection is the deliberate choice here. It sees through smoke, and it reports every day.

Why not dNBR burn severity? Sentinel-2 burn severity needs a clear pre-fire and post-fire pair. Kalimantan's fire season is its cloud season: over a month-long window the optical composite is mostly holes, and the "low severity" class fills up with ordinary vegetation change rather than fire. A severity map that is confidently wrong over half the country is worse than no severity map, so this screen does not produce one.

2 · How burned area is counted

One VIIRS detection pixel is 375 m × 375 m = 0.140625 km². Burned area is the number of distinct pixels that fired at least once during the selected period, times that constant:

$$\text{burned area}\;[\mathrm{km^2}] \;=\; N_{\text{distinct pixels}} \;\times\; 0.140625$$

Counting distinct pixels, not detections, is what makes the number an area rather than a tally of overpasses: a pixel that burns for five days in a row is one burned pixel, not five.

What this figure is, and is not. It is the standard hotspot-footprint proxy, and this tool never calls it a burn perimeter. It over-states a small fire — a single smouldering hectare still lights a whole 375 m pixel — and it under-states a large one where a burn scar sat under cloud at every overpass and was never detected. It is a consistent, daily, national indicator of where fire is concentrated. It is not a mapped burn area, and it should not be quoted as one.

3 · The forest split

A burned pixel counts as forest when the in-house Random-Forest land-cover map (2025, 30 m) says forest at that pixel's centre. That gives each city two figures — total burned area, and the part of it that was forest — plus the share of the city's own forest that has gone.

Total forest area per Kabupaten is sampled, not summed: one 30 m sample per 1 km cell. A systematic sample is unbiased for an area total and costs a fraction of reading every 30 m pixel of the country. The trade is visible noise in small Kabupaten, and the table on the screen says so where it matters.

4 · The period control

One control at the top of the left column governs everything below it: a picker holding the live 30-day window plus one entry per year, and a slider whose twelve stops are January to December.

The map layer, the KPI row, the province ranking, the table and the daily chart all follow it together. A screen where the map shows July while the numbers beside it show "the last 30 days" is a screen that gets misread, so that state cannot be reached. Only changing the year costs a server call; moving the slider re-reads numbers already in the browser.

5 · Reading the hotspot colours

The ramp runs violet → red → orange → yellow, newest first. In the live window the four classes are: under 1 day, 1–3 days, 3–7 days, and 7 days or older. Within a past month, the same four colours split that month into quarters, so the map reads identically and only the legend's wording changes.

Why violet for "burning now". Red is the obvious choice and the wrong one: dark red-brown burn scars and bare peat are exactly what surrounds a fresh detection, so red hotspots disappear into their own background. Violet is the one hue that does not occur in an optical scene of Indonesia — not in forest, burn scar, cloud, smoke or water — so the pixels burning right now cannot be mistaken for anything else. The oldest class ends at yellow rather than grey, because grey reads as "no data" over satellite imagery, and a week-old detection is data.

6 · Honesty about the feed

The daily chart stops at the last day LANCE has published, not at today. The near-real-time feed runs a day or more behind, and a trailing point at hard zero reads as "the fires stopped" when it actually means "nobody has looked yet". Truncating is the honest rendering.

The screen also survives an empty feed. A LANCE outage, or a month older than the rolling archive, returns no images at all; every reduction is written to produce an empty result rather than an error, so the screen reports nothing found instead of breaking.

7 · What it costs to run

Three server reductions, and that is the entire budget: the fire time series (day, month and forest split per city, for the selected year), total forest area per city, and burned area per year across six years — kept separate precisely so that moving the year picker does not re-read all six. Everything after that — rendering, clicking, switching period, building the CSV — is client-side, the same rule the flood dashboard follows.

The time series come back as histogram dictionaries rather than one band per step. A whole year of per-day, per-month, forest-split burned area is twelve bands, because each month's band encodes day-of-month and the forest flag together and one histogram decodes all of it. Spelled naively that is 12 × 31 × 2 bands.

Deliberately absent: damage value, affected population, building counts, burn severity and deforestation history. Each is a further national pass for a number this screen does not need in order to answer where it is burning and how much forest is going.
