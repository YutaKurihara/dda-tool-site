User Manual

This manual explains how to use the DDA Tool to map disaster-affected areas, estimate direct physical damage, and prepare disaster assessment outputs. Sections 1–8 are the operating procedure; the appendices hold the script map, the Asset layout and the calculation details. The science behind each hazard lives on its own page — Flood, Landslide, Earthquake, Forest Fire.

1 · Overview

The tool supports flood, landslide and earthquake damage assessment. Separate functions are available for active fire monitoring and burned area mapping. For flood, landslide, earthquake and burned-area assessments the workflow consists of three main stages:

  1. Detect disaster-affected areas using satellite images.
  2. Estimate damage using exposure and cost data.
  3. Review the results and export reports and GIS files.

Under the hood it is a Google Earth Engine implementation of satellite-based post-disaster damage assessment for BNPB. It detects the footprint of a hazard from Sentinel-1/2 imagery, overlays that footprint on 38 curated exposure layers, and prices the direct physical damage in rupiah following the Jitupasna guideline (Perka BNPB No. 15/2011) and the reporting items of the DIBI database. Everything runs on Earth Engine; no local GIS software is needed to produce an estimate.

Fire is the one hazard with two screens. The active fire monitor reads the daily VIIRS thermal feed for all 514 districts, stores nothing and values nothing, because a fire season is a stream rather than an event. The burned area mapping script is a Stage 1 detector like the other three: it maps what burned from a Sentinel-1 time series and, since 2026-10-17, its result goes through Stage 2 and the dashboard like a landslide.

Two words to keep apart. The tool estimates direct physical damage (Jitupasna kerusakan): exposed assets × replacement unit cost × damage ratio. It does not estimate indirect economic losses (kerugian — lost income, business interruption). Its CSVs are preliminary assessment tables formatted to support DIBI and Jitupasna reporting; additional information and field verification may be required for official submission — casualties, chronology and demographic breakdowns cannot be observed from satellite and are left blank.

2 · Quick Start

Important: creating an export task does not start the calculation. Each task must be started from the Earth Engine Tasks tab by pressing RUN. This is the step people forget, at every stage.
Step 1 — Detect the disaster
  1. Open the detection script for the hazard.
  2. Select the disaster date and the affected area.
  3. Review the preview map.
  4. Press Export.
  5. Open the Tasks tab and RUN the export tasks.
  6. Confirm that all required exports have completed.
Step 2 — Estimate damage
  1. Open the Damage Calculation script.
  2. Refresh the event list.
  3. Select the completed disaster event.
  4. Run the damage estimation.
  5. RUN the tasks in the Tasks tab.
  6. Confirm that the results have been saved.
Step 3 — Review and export
  1. Open the Dashboard.
  2. Select the disaster event.
  3. Review the damage estimates and affected areas.
  4. Share the new Assets so the published app can read them.
  5. Export DIBI or Jitupasna tables as needed.
  6. Export GIS data for further analysis when required.

3 · Requirements

  • An Earth Engine account with access to the Cloud project that owns the Assets. Reading the published dashboard needs nothing; running the pipeline needs write access.
  • The scripts, in the Code Editor. Three folders, numbered in dependency order: 01_shared/ (the library everything else requires), 02_event/ (the per-event work — Stage 1 and Stage 2), 03_dashboard/ (Stage 3). See Appendix A.
  • The Code Editor, not the published app. A published Earth Engine App cannot queue Export tasks. The dashboard's CSV downloads work in the app (they use a direct download URL), but every Asset export and every GIS export must be started from the Code Editor.
Two clocks to keep in mind. Anything computed on screen is killed by Earth Engine after about five minutes, on every billing plan. Anything computed as a batch export has no such limit. The whole tool is arranged around that split: the expensive work happens once, in batch, and every screen afterwards only reads the result. A province-wide valuation is a multi-hour batch task — start it and come back.

4 · Step 1 — Detect the disaster

Open the script for the hazard you are assessing. The detectors share the same panel layout and the same workflow; only the detection parameters differ, and those are documented on the hazard pages.

  1. Event ID. A short slug, e.g. jakarta_2025_nov. The script adds the hazard prefix and the export date for you, so what actually gets written is flood_jakarta_2025_nov_20260824. That full string is the event id and it is the key every later stage uses. The date stamp means re-running the same event on a later day never collides with the earlier result.
  2. Area of interest. Three ways: pick a province and a kabupaten/kota; pick a province and "(Whole province)"; or leave the province blank and draw a polygon on the map.
  3. Parameters. Dates, thresholds, sensor mode — see the hazard page.
  4. Preview. Renders the result on a coarser grid so it stays inside the interactive time limit. A province-wide preview is deliberately coarse and indicative; the export is the real thing. Use the preview to check that you have the right dates, the right area, and a plausible footprint — not to read areas off the screen.
  5. Export. Queues the Asset tasks — two for a flood (the observed extent and the depth), two for a landslide (the extent and the potential road blockages), one for an earthquake, four for a fire (the scar, its status raster, the area table and its CSV). Nothing runs until you open the Tasks tab and press RUN on each one. For a province-wide flood start the depth task first: it is the long one, and Earth Engine runs about two tasks at a time.

What each detector writes

HazardAssetsTasks
Flood floodmask_<id> (observed extent, 10 m), flooddepth_<id> (depth + depth_flag, 30 m)2
Landslide landslidemask_<id>, roadblock_<id>2
Earthquake quakemask_<id>1
Fire (burned area) burnmask_<id>, burnseverity_<id>, burnarea_<id> — plus a Drive CSV of the area table. Stage 2 needs the first two.4

For floods the reported extent is the observed flood plus the pixels the depth model infers to be flooded — terrain below the water surface inside areas the radar could not decide (see Flood). That union is made when the Assets are read, so the mask task stays a light Sentinel-1 job and lands first. Until the depth task has landed, the dashboard shows the observed extent and flags DEPTH PENDING; run Stage 2 only once both Assets exist, or the valuation will cover the observed extent alone.

Only floods get a depth raster. For landslides, earthquakes and fires, assets within the detected area are assumed to be completely destroyed for preliminary damage estimation; actual damage must be verified separately. For fires the engine also leaves out roads, power lines and annual cropland (Forest Fire, Part B).

roadblock_<id> is a point per contiguous stretch where the slide crosses a national, regional or toll road — a possible road blockage, marked × on the dashboard. It is a satellite-detected overlap, not a confirmed closure; ground confirmation remains the operator's job.

5 · Step 2 — Estimate damage

Open 02_event/04_damage_calculation.js.

  1. Refresh the list and choose your event. An event only appears once its Stage-1 Assets have finished exporting — for a flood that means both the mask and the depth raster, for a fire both the scar and its status raster. If it is missing, your task is still running (or you never pressed RUN).
  2. Choosing it draws the extent and the district boundaries, so you can confirm you are about to value the right footprint.
  3. Press "Value everything and store the result". This queues the batch task (two for an earthquake). RUN them.
  4. Come back later. A single kabupaten finishes in minutes; a whole province can take hours. When the task is done, press "Refresh the list" and select the event again — the numbers appear.
AssetContents
damage_value_<id> The valuation. One row per district × damage class, plus facility counts, housing damage grades and population indicators. This is what the dashboard and both CSVs are built from.
affectedbld_<id> Earthquakes only: the whole building footprints the hazard touched. Feeds the dashboard's Housing overlay. Queued separately so the numbers land first; the dashboard works without it, minus that one layer.

What the engine computes — quantity × unit cost × damage ratio × province ratio, per pixel — is set out in Appendix C. The same run produces the Sendai Framework indicators: affected facility counts (Target D), the damaged / destroyed housing split (C-4), and population (B-1, B-3, B-4). Every number can be traced to its inputs on the Data Check page.

If a province-wide export fails, the first switch to try is housingCount: false in the Stage-2 call. It drops only the estimated house counts and keeps every rupiah figure, facility count, population indicator and housing floor area.

6 · Step 3 — Review and export

Open 03_dashboard/01_dashboard.js, or the published dashboard. It never computes a valuation; it reads the stored table, which is why it opens in seconds however large the event was.

6.1 Reading the dashboard

  • Event selector. Only events that still have both their valuation table and their hazard extent are listed. If a valuation is hidden, the Console says which and why — usually its Stage-1 mask was deleted.
  • KPI row — total estimated direct damage, districts affected, people, houses, area, hardest-hit district.
  • Charts — damage by sector, top damage classes, top districts.
  • Map — hazard extent, per-district damage choropleth, flood depth (floods only), exposure overlays, the × potential-road-blockage markers where a roadblock_ Asset exists, district names drawn on the map, and a Village boundaries layer (off by default — 83,000 villages nationally, drawn for the event area only). Clicking a district also reports the village under the cursor.
  • Click any district to re-scope the class-totals table to it. "← All cities" returns to the event-wide view.
  • Badges. DEPTH PENDING (flood depth not yet available — observed extent only) and STATUS PENDING (fire status raster not yet available — all detections shown) mean the event is not ready for Stage 2.

6.2 The two CSVs

ButtonFormat
Export DIBI CSV The per-year Data Bencana schema — 98 Indonesian-named columns in the published order, one row per district. Columns the tool measures start at numeric 0; everything satellite cannot observe (casualties, chronology, demographic breakdowns) is left blank, the same convention the published file uses. Money is summed into kerugian_juta (millions of rupiah). Tool-specific additions are appended after the official 98 with an x_ prefix.
Export Jitupasna CSV Long format: one row per district × damage class, with quantity, unit cost, effective damage ratio and rupiah. This is the file to use when you need to see how a number was reached.

Both appear as a green download link in the header when ready. They are direct downloads, not Tasks, so they also work from the published app. They are preliminary assessment tables formatted to support DIBI and Jitupasna reporting, not complete official reports.

6.3 GIS download — the hazard extent and the exposed features

The right column has Export to GIS (Drive). One press queues one batch for the loaded event: the hazard extent as polygons — with area_ha and the district each patch falls in, which is what a patch-size check needs — and, one task per layer, every exposure layer filtered to the features that intersect that extent. The two belong together: every exposed-feature file was cut against that extent.

  1. Load an event.
  2. Choose the vectorisation grid — 10 m (native), 30 or 100 m. The polygons are built one district at a time so the job stays inside a worker, but a fragmented province-wide mask at 10 m is still the heaviest task here; drop to 30 or 100 m when only the shape of the extent is in question.
  3. Choose a format — GeoJSON (default), SHP, CSV or KML. GeoJSON is the safe default: shapefiles are capped at 2 GB and 255 columns, which the housing layer can exceed.
  4. Press Queue Drive exports.
  5. Open the Tasks tab and RUN them. Files arrive in Google Drive under DDA_<event id>.

Exported polygons are the whole footprints of the affected features, never clipped to the hazard extent — a half-flooded hospital is exported as a whole hospital, with an exposed column marking it. The housing layer is read from affectedbld_<id> when Stage 2 has produced it. Batch exports have no size limit, but this is Code Editor only.

6.4 Publishing the Assets — the sharing button

Why this step exists. A published Earth Engine App reads Assets as whoever opens it, not as whoever published it. Until the Assets are public, the app works only for you. And it cannot be folded into the export: Export.*.toAsset has no sharing parameter, and could not use one, because the Asset does not exist until the task finishes. Publishing is always a second pass.
Before sharing results. Check which datasets and outputs will become publicly accessible. Do not publish restricted, confidential or third-party datasets without the required permissions. Verify applicable data licences and sharing conditions before changing Asset access settings.

The right column has Share BNPB Assets (visible only to the owner of the Assets — the app hides it from visitors, who could not use it anyway).

  1. Press once. It walks the whole BNPB tree and reports how many Assets are still private, listing them in the Console. Nothing has changed yet.
  2. Read the list and confirm nothing on it is restricted.
  3. Press again to publish them. Each write is read back from the server before it is counted, so the reported figure is what is actually true of the Assets, not what the call attempted.

Run it after every round of exports. Already-public Assets are never rewritten, and the owner plus any individual reader you granted are carried through untouched. Folders are published too — a private folder cannot be listed by anyone else, so publishing its contents while leaving the directory shut would be half a job.

Licences travel with the data. The OSM-derived exposure layers (worship, markets, offices, shops, hotels, fuel, factories, clinics) are ODbL: publishing them obliges you to attribute OpenStreetMap and keep derivatives open. The flood-depth rasters derive from FABDEM, CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 — attribution, share-alike, and non-commercial. Publishing is compatible with that licence; commercial use is not. Say so wherever you link the data.

7 · Data verification

The Data Check screen (01_shared/07_datacheck.js) renders every input the valuation depends on, before you trust a number built on it. It does no server-side computation, so it opens instantly.

  • Unit Costs — every value with its source reference.
  • DDF Curves — each curve plotted, plus a depth-vs-ratio table.
  • Province Ratio — the BPS IKK 2025 multipliers.
  • Crops & Fish — per-m² production values.
  • Exposure — every layer with its producing ministry, feature count and the DIBI column it feeds. At the bottom, Verify every Asset probes all 44 source Assets — the 38 exposure layers, the four boundary levels, the land cover and the population raster — and reports each as present or NOT FOUND. Run this after any Asset is added, renamed or moved — a missing source Asset does not degrade quietly, it stops the whole valuation.
  • Sanity Test — a worked example: one house at 1.5 m of water, priced by hand, then repeated across provinces.
  • Engine Test — checks the per-pixel damage curve against the reference implementation on every curve, and guards the population arithmetic against the resolution trap: the population raster is a 30 m people-per-pixel grid, and summing it on the 10 m hazard grid would count every person nine times.

Passing these checks confirms that the datasets are present and that the calculations behave as expected. It does not guarantee that every input is current, complete or accurate; review sources, edition dates and known limitations before an official use.

Appendix A · The scripts

The folder numbers are the dependency order, which is why they read 01 → 02 → 03: the shared library comes first because everything else requires it, the per-event scripts come next, and the dashboard last because it only reads what they produced.

You only ever open six of these — the four detectors, the dashboard and the fire monitor, plus Data Check when you want to inspect the inputs. Everything in 01_shared/01–06 is library code that the others require(); you edit it when a cost or a threshold changes, never to run an assessment.

01_shared/02_unit_costs.js Library — Indonesian unit costs at 100 % damage, and the per-province construction-cost ratios.
01_shared/03_damage_curves.js Library — the depth–damage functions, and the per-pixel evaluation the engine uses.
01_shared/04_damage_classes.js Library — the catalog of what gets valued: each damage class with its exposure layers, curve, unit cost and DIBI column. Adding a class is a data edit here.
01_shared/05_flood_depth.js Library — the FwDET water-depth algorithm. Used by the flood detector only.
01_shared/06_damage_calc.js Library — the damage engine. Every rupiah figure, facility count, housing grade and population indicator comes out of here, for all four hazards.

The three stages communicate only through Earth Engine Assets. That is why adding a hazard needs no change to the valuation engine or the dashboard — which is exactly how landslide, earthquake and burned-area support were added.

events/ was called flood/ until floods stopped being the only hazard. Retired data (the superseded DEMNAS 8 m mosaics) and debug leftovers live outside BNPB, because dead weight inside the live tree reads as live. The boundaries were BPS 2020 (via OCHA) until 2026-10-05 and the population WorldPop 2020 until 2026-10-06; both were replaced by the Indonesian government sources above.

Two codings for the same district. The boundaries are identified by Kemendagri codes (ADM2_PCODE, e.g. ID3171 = Jakarta Pusat). DIBI still uses the older BPS codes, and the two disagree for 199 of the 514 districts — not as missing codes but as the same number pointing at a different place (BPS 3171 is Jakarta Selatan). The CSVs the tool publishes therefore carry the BPS code in city_id, read from the boundary's DIBI_PCODE attribute, so they join DIBI as before. Never join DIBI on ADM2_PCODE.

Naming is positional and every stage relies on it: <prefix>_<event id>, where the prefix is one of floodmask_, flooddepth_, landslidemask_, quakemask_, roadblock_, damage_value_, affectedbld_ — and for fire burnmask_, burnseverity_, burnarea_. The event id itself starts with flood_, landslide_, quake_ or fire_, and that prefix is what tells the engine which hazard it is looking at.

Appendix C · What the engine computes

For every damage class, at each 10 m pixel:

$$\text{damage}_{\text{IDR}} \;=\; \sum_{\text{pixels}} \Big[\, \text{quantity} \times \text{unit cost}_{100\%} \times \mathrm{DDF}(\text{depth}) \,\Big] \times \text{province ratio}$$
  • Quantity — exposed area (m² or ha) or length (km) in that pixel.
  • Unit cost — full reconstruction cost at 100 % damage, from Indonesian government price standards (DKI SHST 2025, HSPK, PUPR) and statistics. Every value is tagged with its source.
  • DDF — depth–damage function (JRC, Huizinga et al. 2017, Asia set, for the classes it covers). Evaluated per pixel, not on a district average, because the curves are non-linear.
  • Province ratio — regional construction-cost index (BPS IKK 2025, DKI Jakarta = 1.00). Not applied to the handful of classes whose unit cost is already a national average.
  • Depth for mask-only hazards — landslides, earthquakes and fires have no depth raster; the engine synthesises a constant 10 m inside the extent, at which every curve has saturated at 100 %. That is how the total-loss assumption is implemented without special-case code downstream.

The result is direct physical damage (kerusakan). The same run also produces the Sendai Framework indicators: affected facility counts (Target D — a facility counts when the area-weighted mean depth over its polygon reaches 0.30 m), the damaged / destroyed housing split (C-4, cut on the housing curve at 30 % and 70 %), and population (B-1, B-3, B-4, from the InaRISK grid by area fraction; earthquakes derive population from building counts instead).
