Potential Building Collapse Detection

The tool uses Sentinel-1 radar and Sentinel-2 optical images to identify buildings that may have collapsed during an earthquake. It compares satellite observations before and after the earthquake to detect changes associated with severe building damage. The results provide an initial indication of potentially collapsed buildings and can help prioritise field inspections.

What the tool provides

  • Maps of areas showing significant satellite-observed change after an earthquake.
  • Identification of buildings potentially affected by collapse.
  • Preliminary estimates of potentially collapsed building area and the associated direct physical damage (reconstruction cost).
  • Information to support field verification and emergency response.

The words may have collapsed, potentially and preliminary are used deliberately. This is a system that extracts places where collapse is suspected so that field verification can be targeted, not a system that confirms collapse.

How it works

  1. Establish pre-earthquake conditions. Satellite images collected during the year before the earthquake are used to determine the normal radar and optical appearance of every pixel, and how much it normally varies.
  2. Detect changes. The first post-earthquake observations are compared with the pre-earthquake conditions. Because the comparison is scaled by the pre-event variability only, it works from the very first post-event image — no waiting for a stack of after-images, no training data.
  3. Identify potential collapse. Building pixels showing unusually large changes are flagged as possible building collapse locations.
  4. Estimate damage. Flagged building footprints are combined with building data to estimate potentially collapsed building area and the reconstruction cost, assuming total loss.

Accuracy and limitations

In the 2018 Palu earthquake validation, the default setting achieved a precision of 24 % and a recall of 62 %. This means the method identified approximately 62 % of the reference-positive cases, but many of the flagged detections did not match the reference damage classification. Part of that is the rarity of collapse: with about 3 % of buildings affected, even a small false-alarm rate on the remaining 97 % produces more false flags than true ones. The method is intended to support rapid screening and field inspection, not to confirm building collapse.

  • Buildings graded destroyed are recovered at about 0.7 (0.85 where cloud-free optical data exist); buildings with partial damage and an intact roof are mostly not separable from intact ones at 10 m. Read the counts as potentially collapsed buildings, not as all damaged houses.
  • What is detectable depends on how buildings fail. Ground that was wiped clean (liquefaction flows, tsunami) is seen clearly; masonry walls that fail while the roof stays in place are largely invisible from above — to radar and to high-resolution optical interpretation alike.
  • The method needs a year of pre-event satellite coverage of the same area. Post-event, one Sentinel-1 pass is enough; optical images add skill only where they are cloud-free.
  • Building footprints come from a national dataset that predates the event; buildings missing from it cannot be flagged.
  • Damage estimates assume total loss inside the flagged footprints and require field verification.

Technical details

For analysts. The detector is 02_event/03_earthquake_detection.js; the statistic is RV-PWTT, a reference-variance variant of the pixel-wise t-test (PWTT) popularised by Ballinger's damage assessments in Ukraine and Gaza.

T1 · The statistic

For each pixel and each channel, a one-year pre-event stack gives a mean \( m_{\text{pre}} \) and standard deviation \( s_{\text{pre}} \); the post-event images give \( m_{\text{post}} \). The change statistic is

$$T \;=\; \frac{\lvert\, m_{\text{pre}} - m_{\text{post}} \,\rvert} {\,s_{\text{pre}} \cdot \sqrt{ \tfrac{1}{n_{\text{pre}}} + \tfrac{1}{n_{\text{post}}} }\,}$$

The denominator uses the pre-event spread alone — "reference variance", the RV in RV-PWTT. Because the post-event variance never enters, the statistic is meaningful from the very first post-event image. That is the property that matters in early response: no waiting for a stack of after-images, no training data, no damage labels.

  • SAR channels — Sentinel-1 VV and VH per relative orbit, Lee-filtered; \(T_{\text{sar}}\) is the maximum over channels.
  • Optical channels (optional) — six Sentinel-2 spectral/texture features, cloud-masked per pixel (Cloud Score+ cs_cdf ≥ 0.6, scenes < 40 % cloud); \(T_{\text{opt}}\) is the maximum over features.
  • Reference period — one year before the event (a full seasonal cycle), with at least 10 SAR and 15 optical scenes so that \(s_{\text{pre}}\), the only variance in the statistic, is well determined. Post-event window: the first two months.

T2 · Decision rule and operating points

A building pixel is flagged as potential collapse when either statistic exceeds its threshold (OR rule). A pixel whose optical side is lost to cloud falls back to the SAR test alone, so the map never has cloud holes. The verdict is deliberately binary — flagged / not flagged, no intermediate grade.

T3 · Sensor modes

  • SAR + Optical — the calibrated fusion (OR rule). Optical adds skill only where cloud-free scenes exist; in the wet season, or for events before Sentinel-2 Level-2A coverage, it falls back to SAR.
  • SAR only — lighter and available from the first Sentinel-1 pass, with its own calibrated thresholds (table above).

T4 · Display and valuation

The preview shows a smoothed \(T_{\text{sar}}\) heat map — the satellite change indicator — wherever \(T_{\text{sar}} \geq 2.7\), the 99 % significance point, and the binary potential collapse areas that Stage 2 values as a separate toggleable layer. The export is a mask only (quakemask_<id>); the valuation engine applies the total-loss convention — flagged footprint area × unit cost × 100 % — and building overlap happens in the damage engine, not in the detector.
